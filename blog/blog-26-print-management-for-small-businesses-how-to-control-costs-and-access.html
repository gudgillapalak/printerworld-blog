Print Management for Small Businesses: How to Control Costs and Access

Learn how small businesses can control printing costs, manage access, improve document security and build a practical print policy.

Care & tips·7 min read·Guide 26 of 50

Introduction: Why Printing Needs a Plan

Printing can seem like a small cost for your business but can quickly add up once you consider the cost of your devices, ink/toner, paper, servicing, electricity and time spent. Having no control over printing can cause unnecessary waste due to duplicated prints, abandoned prints, colour printing for drafts and print left in output trays.

Print management helps manage how your staff use your printers, whilst also ensuring you’re paying for, securing and maintaining your printers correctly. You don’t need to invest in enterprise print management. By setting up clear defaults, sensible access and having a simple inventory system you can start to make your print more reliable, easier to use and reduce unnecessary costs as well as keeping your information secure.

1. Know what you’re currently spending on printing

Start by creating a list of all your printers and multifunction devices. List out what model they are, where they are located, how old they are, how they’re connected, approx. monthly print volume and what type of ink/supplies they take. Be sure to include printers in the reception area, home offices and departments.

Look at your one-offs and recurring costs. Recurring can include ink cartridges, toner, paper, servicing kits, repairs and servicing agreements. Think about the costs associated with time spent dealing with jams, or if your printer is far away. Just because your printer was cheap to buy doesn’t mean it’s cheap to run.

If possible look at each device and see what their print counter or usage is. This will show you how much your staff are really using the printers and if any are being used heavily. A simple spreadsheet can often be used for small businesses. Be sure to note down what date the information is from and where it came from.

2. Choose the right printers for your business

Depending on what you’re printing, you’ll need different printers. If you have a small office and just printing invoices and internal documents a reliable black and white laser printer will be fine. However, if you’re a design studio, photographer or marketing team you’ll need colour printing and specialised media. If you’re regularly scanning, copying and printing then a multifunction printer could be useful.

Don’t go for the biggest, fastest printer as it looks more professional. Think about what your monthly volume is, the duty cycle, paper sizes, duplex printing, network features, scanning requirements and what your ink/supplies cost and yield. Check the manufacturers specs to ensure it’s suitable for what you expect to print. If you’re unsure how much you’ll be printing, estimate a realistic range.

3. Set printer defaults

Setting printer defaults can help control how your staff print. Set your printers to print in black and white/grayscale, duplex and standard/draft quality for regular printing. This can be overridden if they really need to print in colour or high quality. Set your printers to print on plain paper and specialised paper only when needed for example for presentations, labels and photographs.

This doesn’t mean you cannot print in colour. If your customer proposal, product image or branded document needs to be in colour then you can. Just ensure your policy explains when it’s appropriate to override defaults and how. Test with some sample documents to ensure text is still readable and duplex printing isn’t causing issues with forms or official documents.

4. Set access levels

You won’t need everyone to have full access to your printers. In a small organisation give access to shared printers by department/location. Only allow someone with authorisation to change the admin settings. Where possible, set up passwords for your printer configuration menu. Ensure ex-employees are removed from the printers.

If your printer has scanning, address book or cloud options only set these to what you need. Don’t leave your printer open for others to access the web admin. Secure your printer on the network and follow the manufacturers guide for firmware updates and account management. Keep it simple so your staff aren’t constantly needing support from IT.

5. Keep your confidential documents safe

Even though your document is secure digitally, it could be exposed when printed. Ensure sensitive information such as payroll, customer info, contracts and internal financial reports are not left in the output tray. Place your shared printers in the correct areas and encourage staff to pick up their documents straight away.

If your printer allows secure printing/PIN code release you may want to enable this for sensitive documents. The user sends their job and then releases it once they’ve authenticated. In smaller offices without this, your staff will only be able to print sensitive documents if they’re nearby and will pick them up straight away. Ensure secure disposal of any misprints or outdated records. Follow your organisations retention and privacy policies.

6. Reduce waste

Encourage your employees to use the print preview function, check they’re printing the right pages and remove any unnecessary pages. Often drafts can be reviewed digitally. If you’re having a meeting consider sending a digital agenda and only printing what is really required. If you need hard copies ensure you’re using duplex and not printing unnecessary coloured backgrounds.

Ensure there is an easy place to recycle single sided waste paper that can be used for internal notes. Do not use paper with confidential information or that could cause issues with the printer. Hold a short team meeting to explain why it makes sense to reduce print but not reuse. Try to make it easy to follow with clear instructions near your shared printers. Where possible set defaults centrally.

7. Monitor your printer usage

Check your printer counters and ink/supply purchases monthly or quarterly. See if there are any changes to the total print volume, colour printing, repeated jobs, servicing and cost per page. If you have a busy month with a large marketing campaign it makes sense that colour printing would increase.

If you have more than one printer, print reports can show you if any printers are becoming too busy. Moving your printer or changing defaults may make it easier to use and reduce costs. Don’t ask for too much information from your employees. Ensure you’re clear on why you’re monitoring and use it to improve your processes.

8. Print management software?

Print management software can help manage your printing by allowing user authentication, print limits, cost allocation, reporting, secure printing and centralised management. This could be useful if you have multiple locations, lots of staff or need to keep your printing secure. However, this comes with subscription, setup and support costs.

If you have a small team and only one or two devices a simple print policy with regular meter checks may be sufficient. Before investing in print management software, think about what issue you need to solve. Does it support your current printer models, operating systems and network? How does it handle user data, logging and access? Does the vendor offer the right support?

9. Regular servicing

Having a servicing schedule will help reduce any disruptions. Ensure you have your printers in a clean and well ventilated area. Use the right paper and follow the servicing instructions for your specific model. Keep track of the ink cartridge and toner models so you know what you need to buy in an emergency. Keep a reasonable level of essential supplies based on your actual usage, storage instructions and lead times from your suppliers.

Don’t overstock ink cartridges. Ink and toner have storage requirements and depending on the product an expiry date/shelf life. Keep track of any regular issues and servicing. If your device is regularly jamming or needing expensive servicing, compare the cost of repairing it vs buying a more suitable model.

10. Create a concise print policy

Your policy can be kept to one page. Include what devices are available, what the defaults are, how to print confidential documents, who can order supplies and who to contact if the printer breaks. Include guidance on colour printing, large print jobs, personal use and secure disposal.

Include why you’re creating the policy to help with reliability, cost, privacy and reduce waste. Keep it simple and update it if you add any new devices or change how you work. A clear policy that is followed will be more useful than a long policy that no one reads.

Conclusion

Print management for small businesses isn’t about stopping people from printing. It’s about ensuring they’re using the correct device, settings and security. Start by taking an inventory and knowing your costs. Choose the right devices for your printing needs, set sensible defaults and keep your confidential printing secure. Regularly monitor your usage and only invest in software if it’s solving a specific issue. This will help make your printing more predictable, cost-effective and secure.
