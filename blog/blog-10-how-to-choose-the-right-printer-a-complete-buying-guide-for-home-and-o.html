How to Choose the Right Printer: A Complete Buying Guide for Home and Office

Compare printer types, running costs, print volume, connectivity and features to choose the right printer for home or office use.

Buying guides·8 min read·Guide 10 of 50

Introduction

Purchasing a printer can seem overwhelming due to there being many models which appear similar. There may be a need for a small printer to help with school projects, forms and occasional photos within a household. A small office may require a printer to deliver fast and reliable print. However, if there are more people within a team, you may require a printer with network capabilities, secure print, scanning and high volumes. Choosing the incorrect printer can result in paying more than necessary upfront, more than expected in consumables and frequent interruptions.

The best way to choose the correct printer for your needs is to firstly think about what you need to print. How many pages do you print per month? Is colour required? What size paper? Do you require scanning or copying? Once you know what you need to print, you can research about specific models. This guide will help you make some key decisions to ensure you get the most value out of your printer.

1. What and how much will you print?

Estimate how many pages you would print per month. This includes all members of the household or staff. Think about whether you have mostly text to print or colour charts, photos, labels and specialist printing. This will really help narrow down your options.

If you are an occasional user, you may want to go for a simple home printer. These will have a small paper tray and wireless. If you're a student you may require a printer which can scan, print and copy. In a small office you may need to think about how many people will be using it, peak times and whether multiple users will be sending print jobs. A high volume printer will be required for a high volume environment. This will have the appropriate paper tray and recommended print volumes.

Some manufacturers will state a maximum duty cycle. This may not necessarily be the amount you should be printing per month. Look for recommended print volumes and compare to your needs. If you purchase a printer which is too small for your printing needs, this can cause wear and frustration. If you purchase a large office printer for occasional home use, this could be a waste of money and space.

2. Inkjet, Ink Tank and Laser

An inkjet printer works by spraying very small amounts of liquid ink onto the paper. There are many different sizes and price ranges. If you're printing colour documents, photos or creative pieces, they can be a good option. Inkjet printers can vary greatly. Some are simple cartridge printers whilst others will be for business or photo printing.

An Ink Tank printer is a type of inkjet printer. Rather than having small replaceable cartridges they have large ink tanks which can be refilled. If you print a lot this can be cheaper per page. However, they can be more expensive to purchase. Work out how much you will be paying for the printer and ink bottles.

A Laser printer uses toner powder and heat to fuse the print. They can be a great option for text heavy printing. Monochrome laser printers can be a cost-effective option if you rarely need colour. However, colour laser printers can be useful for many business graphics. If you're printing photos, an inkjet printer focused on photos may produce better results. Think about what you're printing.

3. Cost

Whilst the initial cost of the printer is important, you need to think about how much you'll be paying for ink/toner, drums (if applicable), servicing parts, paper, electricity and potential repair costs. You should also consider how much you'll be wasting due to incorrect settings, paper jams and constant cleaning of the printhead

Look at the estimated page yield of the correct consumables. Page yields are based on standard testing. Actual page yields will depend on how much is printed on each page, print setting and type of document. If you're printing a page with lots of photos or full colour graphics, this can use a lot more ink/toner than a basic text page. Some laser printers have separate drums whilst others have everything within the cartridge. This will effect how much you pay in the long run.

A good way to work this out is to calculate how much it will cost per page. This can be done by dividing the price by the expected page yield. This is just an estimate. If you're printing regularly, it may be worth paying a little more for a printer with affordable consumables. If you're only printing a few pages per month, you may want a printer with a low price and easy to find consumables.

4. Quality & Speed

When printing letters, projects and invoices clarity is important. Look at sample pages and reviews of the exact model. When printing photos, think about colour quality, photo paper, borderless printing and how many colours of ink. When printing business graphics, think about if small text, fine lines and coloured charts are clear

Speed is usually listed as pages per minute. However, this depends on how they tested it and what print setting they used. If you're printing in draft mode this may be quicker. The first page may take longer to print than the rest. This is because the printer may need to warm up or the print is complex. If speed is important, look at independent testing. Think about how it will perform with your documents.

Look at if it has automatic duplex printing. This can help reduce the amount of paper used when printing reports and longer documents.

5. Paper & Feature

Most home and office printers will be able to print standard A4 paper. However, some may be able to print larger A3 paper, envelopes, labels, photo paper or thick media. Look at what weights of paper it can print and how to feed them. If you're doing any creative work, a printer which can print a variety of media may be useful. However, ensure it can print the correct paper path and settings.

An All-in-One printer will allow you to print, scan and copy. Some can also fax. If you regularly scan multi-page documents look at the resolution of the scanner, automatic document feeder and duplex scanning. An automatic document feeder can be very useful in an office. However, if you're an occasional user at home you may not mind manually feeding pages.

Think about how big the paper tray is. A small paper tray may be okay for home use. However, it may be inconvenient in a busy office. Look at if there are extra paper trays available. Also, ensure the output tray can hold a typical print job without pages falling off.

6. Connectivity

Printers can have a USB, Wi-Fi or Ethernet connection. A USB connection may be suitable if you're only using it with one computer. A Wi-Fi connection makes it easier to share between multiple devices. An Ethernet connection can give a reliable wired connection in an office. Some printers can also be printed to from mobile devices. This can be done via the manufacturers applications or common mobile printing services.

Ensure it is compatible with your computers, phones and operating system. Check that there are drivers and applications available for your operating system. If you're sharing it between your business network, think about how users will find it, manage print queues and security. Not all printers will have all the wireless and cloud printing features.

In a small office, you may need to consider security on the network. If possible change the default admin login details. Ensure you have updated firmware from the official sources. Limit who can use it. Ensure you follow any IT policies your organisation has

7. Size, Noise, Power & Position

Measure out where you're going to put your printer. Ensure you have enough space to open the covers, add paper and remove your finished prints. A small printer may fit on a shelf. However, ensure it has the correct ventilation and the shelf can hold the weight. Multifunction printers will be larger and may be difficult to move.

If you're in a shared home or quiet office, noise can be an issue. Look at reviews to see what it sounds like when printing and scanning. Power usage can also be an issue if it's left on all day. Look for sleep modes and automatic power saving features. Ensure they fit with your workflow.

Place your printer on a stable and even surface. Avoid areas with too much dust, moisture or direct heat. Ensure there is ventilation. Store your paper and consumables in suitable conditions.

8. Warranty, Consumables & Service

Before you purchase look at what warranty the manufacturer offers. How easy is it to service? Are the consumables available? If the printer is attractive in price, it may be inconvenient if the cartridges aren't easy to source or there isn't much local support. Ensure you know the exact model number and consumable codes. Similar looking printers may have different consumables.

Look at what is covered in the warranty. Is it parts and labour? How easy is it to service? Will it need to be sent to a service centre? If it is for business use, it may be important to know how quickly it can be serviced and if there are spare parts available. Keep your invoice, warranty and product information.

Not all 3rd party cartridges/toners will be suitable. If you're considering compatible consumables, look at what the printer manufacturer says, what the supplier says is compatible and what is applicable to your device. The safest option is to use something which states it is compatible with your exact model.

Conclusion

Choose a printer which suits your actual printing volume, documents, budget and support. Think about inkjet, ink tank and laser options. Work out how much you'll be paying for consumables. Think about speed and quality. Look at paper, scanning, connectivity and warranty. A bit of planning can help avoid unnecessary costs and hassle over years.

Write down how many pages you expect to print per month and what features you need. Compare a few exact models using the same criteria.
